Six faculty stories

The same platform, six very different asks — from a fourteen-person seminar tool built in an afternoon to a chatbot answering five thousand students. Each story is here because it hits something the others do not.

These people are invented. The situations are the ones the design has to handle.
01

Reading roulette

Art History. Runs a graduate seminar of fourteen. Wants every student to present a different reading each week, wants it to feel fair, and is tired of doing it in a spreadsheet at eleven at night.

  • 14 people
  • no sign-in
  • tiny database
  • no AI
  • Manifest address
  • simplest possible
  • Tuesday afternoonSigns in with their CWL and types one sentence: “A page where I paste my seminar reading list and it assigns each student a different one each week, without repeats.”
  • Twenty minutes laterA working version is running at a practice address they can click. They paste last term’s reading list and find the shuffle puts two long articles back to back. They say so, in the chat. It changes.
  • ThursdayTwo colleagues try it on their own seminars and nothing breaks. They ask to go live.
  • The launch checkManifest points out that with no sign-in, anybody who has the link can change the assignments. It offers to add CWL sign-in, which would mean going through UBC registration first. They decide fourteen people who already know each other do not need it, and record that choice — which is now on the record rather than an unexamined assumption.
  • FridayLive. No paperwork, because with no sign-in there is no personal information and no registration to do.
Live at
reading-roulette.manifest.apps.ltic.ubc.ca
Size
Me and a few colleagues — one small copy that sleeps when nobody is using it
Start to live
Three days, most of it waiting for colleagues to try it. No UBC review needed at all
Shows
The floor. No sign-in, no paperwork, no waiting — and a guardrail that informs rather than blocks
02

Lab bench booking

Chemistry. Coordinates a first-year lab course. 240 students choose one of forty bench slots. Booking opens at 9am on a Monday in September and, historically, the departmental page falls over at 9:01.

  • 240 students
  • all at once
  • CWL sign-in
  • database
  • no AI
  • custom address
  • Week 1Describes it: students sign in, see available benches, take one, can swap until a cutoff. Because it needs to know who booked, it needs CWL sign-in — so this app will need UBC registration, and Manifest says so on day one rather than in August.
  • Week 1–2Builds in the sandbox against fake test students. Discovers by trying it that two people can grab the same bench in the same second, and that gets fixed before anyone real sees it.
  • Week 2 — the size questionManifest asks who this is for. A whole course. Then it asks the second question: do they all arrive at once? Yes — 9am, September, all 240. That second answer is what changes the plan: several copies running, capacity warmed up before the window opens, and nobody arriving at a cold app.
  • Week 2 — the addressThey want chem.ubc.ca/labs. That is a path on a site the department already runs, which Manifest cannot serve — it can answer for a whole name, not a folder on somebody else’s. They take labs.chem.ubc.ca instead. Manifest prints the two DNS records for the departmental IT contact, who creates them that afternoon.
  • Week 2, ordered deliberatelyThe address is settled before the UBC registration is submitted, because the address is written into it. Had they chosen it in August, changing it would have meant going back through that registration a second time, in the week before term.
  • In parallelThe sign-in registration and the privacy assessment go to the colleagues who review them. Manifest drafted both and tracks them from here. Meanwhile the app keeps improving against test students — none of that work is blocked, which is the point of running the two things alongside each other.
  • Once registeredA rehearsal sign-in against UBC’s real test system catches a mismatch in one field name. Twenty minutes to fix in August; a broken launch morning in September.
  • Before launchA load rehearsal: 240 simulated students in sixty seconds. It holds.
  • September, 9amIt holds for real.
Live at
labs.chem.ubc.ca
and permanently also at chem-lab-booking.manifest.apps.ltic.ubc.ca
Size
A whole course, all arriving at once — several copies, warmed ahead of the booking window
Plan for
Started in May for a September course, on purpose. The reviews ran alongside the building rather than after it
Shows
The full path: sign-in, a custom address, a burst of traffic, and the ordering rule that costs a month if you get it wrong
03

Office hours queue

Mathematics. Six instructors across three sections, roughly 180 students. Wants a queue so students stop hovering in the corridor and can see how many people are ahead of them.

  • 180 students
  • steady use
  • CWL sign-in
  • database
  • no AI
  • nearly missed the address
  • Week 1Straightforward to build. Sign in, join the queue, see your position, instructors call the next person.
  • Week 2 — the question worth stopping forAt registration, Manifest asks whether this app will ever live at a departmental address. Their instinct is to say no and get on with it. The warning next to the question is specific: say no now and saying yes later means going back through this registration. So they ask the department head, who says the department has wanted queue.math.ubc.ca for two years.
  • Week 2Address settled first, registration submitted second. Two days of their own delay, instead of going back through registration in November, mid-term, with 180 students already using it.
  • In parallelThe reviews. The privacy assessment is short: the app stores a student number and a timestamp, and Manifest’s draft says so plainly, because it can see exactly what the app asked for.
  • Once approvedLive, in time for midterms.
  • FebruaryAn instructor asks for a “how long have people been waiting on average” view. It ships that week, with no approval needed — it does not touch anything sensitive, so it simply deploys.
  • The following yearThey share it in the showcase. Eleven other courses start from it rather than describing a queue from scratch — each one their own app, with their own students, doing their own review.
Live at
queue.math.ubc.ca
Size
A whole course, steady — two copies, no sleeping during term
Plan for
Built over the summer for a fall course. The reviews set the launch date, not the building
Shows
The platform asking the awkward question at the only moment the answer is cheap — and afterwards, how little friction ordinary updates carry
04

Reading responses

Education. Teaches a 90-student course with weekly written responses to set readings. Wants a tool that reads a student’s response alongside the reading and drafts feedback for the instructor to edit — never sent to the student automatically.

  • 90 students
  • CWL sign-in
  • database
  • AI over documents
  • confidential data
  • wants Canvas
  • Week 1Describes it. This is the first story where the AI is part of the running app rather than just the thing that built it: the app searches the course readings for relevant passages and asks a model to draft feedback grounded in them.
  • Week 1 — the first refusalThey mark the data as confidential, correctly — it is student work with names attached. Manifest immediately narrows the list of AI models the app may use to those running on UBC hardware. The faster, cheaper option is simply not offered. The reason is shown, not just the restriction: a BC public body may not send student personal information to a model running in another country.
  • Week 2 — the second refusalThey ask for the class list to come from Canvas automatically. Manifest cannot do that yet. What it does instead is say so plainly, show that a place is reserved for exactly this, and offer a CSV upload in the meantime. They upload a CSV.
  • Weeks 2–4Building. Costs are visible per student as they test, so the projected termly bill is a number rather than a worry.
  • The review that matteredThe privacy assessment is the most substantial of these six, because the app sends student writing to a language model. Manifest’s draft states which model, where it runs, what it is sent, and how long it is kept. The Privacy Office asks two questions and approves. The instructor did not have to write any of that from scratch, which is the difference between this happening and not happening.
  • Once approvedLive, with a per-student AI spending cap so one pathological essay cannot consume the term’s budget.
Live at
reading-responses.manifest.apps.ltic.ubc.ca — no custom address wanted
Size
A whole course, steady
Plan for
A full term ahead. This one has the most for a reviewer to consider, and that is the right outcome rather than a delay
Shows
Guardrails doing their job twice — once by narrowing a choice with a reason attached, once by refusing clearly instead of half-delivering
05

Field data explorer

Forestry. A research group with one graduate student who codes. They have their own AI assistant set up on their own laptop and no intention of using anybody else’s chat window. They want their field measurements browsable by the eight people on the project.

  • 8 people
  • CWL sign-in
  • database
  • no AI in the app
  • own AI, own laptop
  • never opens the console
  • Day 1Signs in once, in a browser, to create the project and issue themselves a token. That is the only time they open Manifest’s web interface again for the rest of the project.
  • Day 1Their own AI asks Manifest for its instruction pack and learns the conventions — how to declare a database, how to wire up sign-in — without ever running inside Manifest. That pack being published rather than buried inside a sandbox is the whole reason this works.
  • Days 2–9They work entirely in their own editor. Push code, Manifest builds it, they watch the log stream in their terminal, the test version updates. No sandbox: they already have a laptop and an AI, so they need code storage and an interface, which is exactly what they get.
  • Day 10 — the boundaryTheir AI tries to promote the app to live. It cannot, and the design says it never will: that is one of the four actions no AI may take, whoever owns it. Instead a confirmation appears, waiting for a person. They read it, click it, and it proceeds.
  • In parallelThe reviews, same as everybody else. Eight users does not exempt an app from registration when it uses CWL.
Live at
forest-plots.manifest.apps.ltic.ubc.ca
Size
Me and a few colleagues — one small copy that sleeps between field seasons
Plan for
The building took nine days. The reviews set the launch date
Shows
The audience that would never open a chat interface, served by the same platform — and the one wall their AI hits, by design
06

Advising assistant

Medicine. The advising office answers the same 200 questions every September — deadlines, elective rules, forms. They want a chat assistant that answers from the faculty’s own handbook and says “ask an advisor” when it does not know. Roughly 5,000 people, including applicants who have no CWL.

  • ~5,000 people
  • public — no sign-in
  • AI over documents
  • two databases
  • custom address
  • heaviest review
  • Week 1The most complex of these six: the handbook has to be indexed so the assistant can find relevant passages, then a model answers using only those passages, with a link back to the source paragraph.
  • Week 2 — two hard questions at onceThe audience is public, which means no CWL, which means no accountability for who is asking — so this app gets the extra scrutiny reserved for anything unauthenticated. And the questions people type are themselves personal information: “I failed this course, what happens to my standing?” That reshapes the privacy assessment entirely.
  • Week 3Decisions follow from that. Conversations are not retained beyond the session. Only UBC-hosted models are used. Rate limiting at the front door, because a public AI-backed app is an expensive thing to leave unguarded.
  • Weeks 4–9Building, and testing with real advisors who keep finding questions it answers confidently and wrongly. Each one becomes a handbook fix or a refusal rule. This is the longest build of the six and none of it is infrastructure.
  • Before launchAn independent security review, required because it is public. A load rehearsal for 5,000 people.
  • Launch, in stagesReleased to the advising office only, at first. Then one program. Then everyone. The platform makes that staging trivial, which is what makes a cautious rollout the easy option rather than the diligent one.
Live at
advising.med.ubc.ca
Size
Public — several copies with headroom, always awake, watched and paged on
Plan for
A term of work, most of it writing and testing answers rather than building software
Shows
The ceiling. Everything the platform has, all at once — and the reminder that at this scale the hard part is the content, not the hosting

The building is rarely what sets the date

In five of six stories, the app itself was finished well before it could go live. What determines the launch date is the review — the sign-in registration and the privacy assessment, both read by colleagues at UBC. Those steps are human on purpose, and they are not something Manifest can decide.

What Manifest can do is make sure none of that time is spent waiting on it: it drafts both documents from what it already knows about the app, submits them, tracks them, and keeps the building unblocked the whole time. The direction of travel is for the platform to hand them over directly to those teams, which is something we are working towards with them. For now the practical advice for faculty is simple: start a term ahead, and build the review into your plan.

The one that needed no review needed no sign-in

Reading roulette went live in three days because it handles no personal information and needs no CWL. That is a real category — simple tools for small groups, live the same week — and being honest that it exists matters, because otherwise every request looks like a term-long request.

The awkward questions get asked early on purpose

Who is this for. Will they all arrive at once. Will this ever live at a departmental address. How sensitive is the data. What else at UBC does it need to talk to. Every one is cheap to answer at the start and expensive to revisit once students are using it, so the platform is arranged to surface them at the first moment they can be answered.

Two of the six never open the front-end at all

The Forestry student uses their own AI on their own laptop. The Medicine project is mostly content work by advisors. Manifest is a platform with a front-end attached, not a front-end with a platform behind it — and these two stories are why that distinction was worth designing for.
