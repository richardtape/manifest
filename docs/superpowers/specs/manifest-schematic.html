UBC · Manifest

A faculty member describes an app. It gets built, and it goes live.

Manifest is the machinery underneath that sentence. It takes an idea described in plain words, has an AI build it, and then runs it properly on UBC infrastructure — with sign-in, a database, a web address and the paperwork UBC requires. Nobody involved has to know what any of that means.

This page the whole thing, in plain language Status designed, not yet built Full detail a 2,000-line design document, which this summarises

01 — The idea

Two ways in, one platform, three places an app can live

Most faculty will describe what they want in a chat window. Some will bring their own AI assistant and never open ours. Both go through exactly the same machinery, which is what keeps the second group from being second-class.

02 — Start to finish

What actually happens, from signing in to students using it

Following one app the whole way: a Chemistry lab booking tool for 240 students. Every screen below is a mock-up — nothing is built yet — but each one shows a real moment in the design.

  1. Signing in, and an empty shelf

    WHERE manifest.ltic.ubc.ca · signs in with their CWL, same as any UBC system

    There is no account to create and no form to fill in. Manifest recognises them from their CWL and shows them their apps, of which there are none yet.

    The only thing on the screen is an invitation to describe something.

  2. Describing it

    WHERE the same page · a conversation, not a form

    They type what they want. The AI asks the few questions that genuinely change the app, and stays quiet about everything else.

    One of its questions matters more than it looks: does the app need to know who each person is? The answer here is yes — you cannot book a bench anonymously — and that single answer commits the app to CWL sign-in, which commits it to UBC registration, which is why it is asked on day one rather than in August.

  3. Watching it get built

    WHERE a sandbox · a private workspace, visible only to them

    The AI works in a sandbox: its own private space with its own copy of the code, its own database and a live preview. Nothing here is reachable by anyone else, and it disappears when it is no longer needed.

    The faculty member watches, clicks the preview, finds things that are wrong, and says so. Most of the work happens in this loop.

  4. The question about people

    WHERE before sharing it · asked once, by a person, answered by a person

    Before the app leaves the sandbox, Manifest asks who it is for. Not how much computing power it needs — nobody can answer that — but how many people, and whether they all turn up at once.

    That second question is the one that matters. Two hundred students over a week is easy. Two hundred students at 10:03 on a Tuesday, because the address went on a lecture slide, is a different problem entirely.

  5. Colleagues try it

    WHERE chem-lab-booking.manifest.staging.apps.ltic.ubc.ca · reachable from inside UBC only

    One click promotes the sandbox version to a test copy with a real, shareable address. Colleagues sign in with test accounts — not their real CWL, and not real students — and use it properly.

    This copy is deliberately unreachable from outside UBC. That is enforced by which door it is wired to, not by a list of who is allowed in: a list can be edited wrongly and fail silently, whereas a door that is not there simply does not open.

  6. Asking to go live — and meeting the gate

    WHERE the going-live checklist · the only slow part of the whole process

    This is where the process stops being fast, and it is the honest heart of the design. Every live UBC app that knows who its users are needs its own sign-in registration with UBC, and its own privacy assessment. Both are reviewed by people outside the team. Both take weeks. Neither can be skipped or automated away.

    What Manifest does instead is write the first draft of both. It knows what the app asked for, so it can state what personal information is involved and why — which turns a blank form into a document to review and sign.

  7. Live

    WHERE labs.chem.ubc.ca · and permanently also at its Manifest address

    Once the registration and the assessment come back approved, going live is one click and takes seconds — because the package being deployed is the identical one that was tested for the last six weeks.

    From here on, ordinary improvements ship without ceremony. Approval is asked for again only when the app starts asking for something genuinely new: another database, a new outside connection, more personal information about students, a different AI model. Everything else just deploys.

The other way in. Nothing above requires the Manifest console. A graduate student with their own AI assistant on their own laptop can do all of it through the published interface — create the project, build, test, watch the logs — without opening a chat window at all. They sign in once in a browser, and again for the four actions that always need a person: going live, reading a password, raising a spending limit, and adding someone to a project.

Their AI can do everything else and never those four, whoever owns it. That single rule is what makes bringing your own AI safe rather than a hole in the design.

Six faculty stories → follow this same path through six very different apps — from a fourteen-person seminar tool built in an afternoon to a chat assistant answering five thousand people.

03 — Three places

Sandbox, test copy, live

Every app moves through the same three places, and to Manifest they are the same kind of thing with different rules. That sameness is what stops the version you tested differing from the version students get.

 SandboxTest copyLive
Who can reach itOnly youAnyone inside UBC with the linkIts intended audience
Who signs inFake test usersFake test usersReal CWL
How long it lastsHoursAs long as you wantYears
Can the AI run commands in itYesNoNo
Real student dataNeverNeverYes
Custom web addressNoNoOptional
Size settings applyNoNoYes
Approval to get hereNoneNoneThe full gate

The test copy is intentionally small and identical for every app, whatever its eventual size. That keeps iteration cheap — but it does mean a test copy does not tell you how the app behaves under load, so any app expecting a large audience does a separate load rehearsal before going live.

04 — Web addresses

Every app gets an address it keeps forever, and may borrow a nicer one

Addresses look like a cosmetic question and are not. Get the order wrong on an app that uses CWL and it costs a month.

What the addresses look like

WhereAddress
Sandboxchem-lab-booking.manifest.sandbox.apps.ltic.ubc.ca
Test copychem-lab-booking.manifest.staging.apps.ltic.ubc.ca
Live — alwayschem-lab-booking.manifest.apps.ltic.ubc.ca
Live — optionallabs.chem.ubc.ca

The Manifest address never goes away

A departmental address is added alongside the Manifest one, never instead of it. This is not tidiness. Three things are pinned to the permanent address — the UBC sign-in registration, the health checks, and the platform’s own tooling — and none of them should depend on what somebody typed into a domain field last week.

It also decides what happens when things go wrong. If the department restructures its DNS, or the address lapses, or somebody makes a typo, the app carries on working at its ugly address instead of going dark.

Proving the address is yours

A faculty member almost never controls their department’s DNS, so Manifest does not ask them to. It produces the exact two records to forward to whoever does, then watches until they appear. The first points traffic here. The second proves this project was the one authorised to claim the name — without it, one project could take an address that belongs to another.

Once both records are there, the security certificate is issued automatically. There is nothing left to check by then: traffic already arrives.

The mistake that costs a month. If an app uses CWL sign-in, its address is written into its UBC registration — because that is where UBC delivers proof of who just signed in. So the address has to be settled before the registration is submitted.

Choosing one afterwards means going back through that registration: another two to four weeks, usually discovered at the worst possible moment. Manifest therefore asks the question at registration time, with the warning attached, rather than offering it later as a convenience setting. Apps with no sign-in are unaffected and can change address whenever they like.

05 — How many people

Faculty answer who it is for. Manifest works out the rest.

Nobody should be asked how much computing power their app needs. Everybody can answer who is going to use this, and do they all turn up at once.

The answerRoughlyWhat changes for the live app
Just me, or a few colleaguesto 25 One small copy that goes to sleep when nobody is using it and wakes when someone visits. Costs essentially nothing between uses. Backed up weekly.
One courseto 400 Two copies, awake through term, backed up nightly, with basic health monitoring.
A large courseto 5,000 Three or more copies with capacity in reserve, monitored properly with someone paged if it struggles, and a load rehearsal before launch.
Anyone with the linkopen As above, plus abuse protection and an independent security review — because with no sign-in there is nothing holding anyone accountable.

The second question is the one people forget

Two hundred students spread over a week barely registers. Two hundred students at 10:03 on a Tuesday, because the address went on a slide, is a completely different engineering problem with an identical headcount.

When the answer is “all at once”, capacity is warmed up ahead of the window rather than started when the first person arrives. Waking a sleeping app is invisible to one person checking something on a Thursday and is a failed lecture for two hundred — the same feature, right in one case and wrong in the other.

Three things worth knowing

  • It only affects the live app. Sandboxes and test copies are always one small sleepy copy, whatever the answer.
  • A person answers it, not the AI. Capacity costs real money, so it is kept out of the settings file the AI writes.
  • Asking for more needs approval; asking for less does not. Both are recorded with who changed it and when, so “why is this app running three copies” always has an answer.

06 — Blueprints

One starting point now, more later, without starting again

A blueprint is the shape every new app starts from. There is exactly one at first. The work now is making sure the second one is a new folder rather than a rewrite.

Every app Manifest builds starts from a blueprint: a prepared skeleton that already knows how to connect to a database, how CWL sign-in works, how to ask an AI model a question, and how to be packaged up and run. It also carries the written instructions that teach an AI how to build for this platform.

The first blueprint is a TypeScript one, because that is what the team knows and what the existing UBC starter app already uses. But not every app people want to build is a TypeScript app — a research group doing data analysis will want Python, and asking them to use something else is asking them not to use the platform.

The trap being avoided

The easy mistake is to ship one blueprint and say “we will add more later”. What happens then is that assumptions about the one language quietly settle into everything around it — how apps get packaged, how their health is checked, which databases appear in the list — and none of them are written down anywhere. They all surface at once, the week somebody asks for Python.

So each blueprint carries a description of itself: its language, what it can connect to, whether it supports sign-in, what it needs to run. Manifest reads that description and assumes nothing. Describing one blueprint costs a day now; untangling four unwritten assumptions later costs a rewrite.

This is the pattern the whole design follows. Storage of code, the way apps are run, where AI models come from, which databases are on offer — each one is a choice with a defined shape, so a second option can be added without disturbing the first. Manifest starts with one of each. It is built so that none of them is the only one possible.

07 — What apps can touch

The code nobody read

Every app on this platform is written by an AI and reviewed by nobody. That one fact shapes more of the design than anything else. Manifest’s first job is not deployment — it is containment.

An app might be perfectly good. It might have a subtle flaw nobody would spot. It might have been steered somewhere odd by text the AI read along the way — a student’s uploaded PDF, a page it browsed, a note buried in a software package it used. Manifest cannot tell which, so it treats every app as though the worst is true and limits what any of them can reach.

A running app canA running app cannot
Use its own databaseSee any other app, or any other app’s database
Ask the AI models it declaredReach the internet, except to the specific places it said it needed
Sign users in with CWLAsk for personal information it was not registered for
Read the passwords Manifest gave itRead any other app’s
Write to its own logReach Manifest itself, or anything that administers it
Serve its own web addressReach UBC’s internal management systems

Two limits worth spelling out

Apps cannot decide how they are built. Building an app means running the instructions it came with, which is the single most powerful moment in the whole process. Those instructions come from the blueprint, never from the app. The machine that does the building holds no passwords, can reach nothing but the package store, and is destroyed afterwards.

No AI can take a consequential action. Going live, reading a password, raising a spending limit, adding someone to a project — these always require a person who is signed in, clicking. Not because our AI is untrustworthy in particular, but because any AI can be talked into things by text it reads. Written as a rule about AIs rather than a rule about our software, it protects equally against somebody’s own assistant on their own laptop.

Stated plainly: none of this makes an AI-written app safe. It makes the damage one can do bounded and known. That distinction is the honest basis for every conversation with the Privacy Office — the claim is containment, not correctness.

08 — Signing in

Two sign-in systems, and why they must not be confused

Practice apps sign in against a Manifest-run copy with invented users. Live apps sign in against real UBC CWL. They are never the same system, and the practice one never touches a real person’s identity.

 Sandboxes and test copiesLive apps
Signs in againstA Manifest-run sign-in systemReal UBC CWL
The users areInvented test peopleReal staff and students
Setting it up takesUnder a second, automaticTwo to four weeks, by hand, at UBC
If it were broken intoYou could impersonate a fake studentSerious

Registering a live app with UBC is manual, slow, and cannot be automated — that is UBC’s requirement, not a limitation of the platform. Rather than pretending otherwise, Manifest builds the whole design around it: practice sign-in is instant so that daily work is never blocked, and the real registration is treated as a tracked piece of work the platform drafts, submits and chases.

Three things this arrangement buys

  • The practice system is worth little to an attacker. It only ever knows about invented people, so breaking into it gains nothing real.
  • An app cannot say where sign-in results should be delivered. Manifest works that out. Otherwise a mistake, or a manipulated AI, could have proof of somebody’s identity delivered to the wrong place.
  • One rehearsal against UBC’s real test system before launch. An app whose first contact with real CWL is launch day will fail on launch day, and the rehearsal is the only way to find that out while it is still fixable.

09 — The admin view

What the team running Manifest sees

The main screen is not a list of apps. It is the list of things waiting on a person — because the design deliberately routes a specific set of decisions through human judgement, and how long people wait for those is the platform’s real health measure.

The other screens

  • All apps — every app, its owner and department, where it is running, when it last changed, how big it is, what addresses it answers on, and what it has spent on AI.
  • People — everyone using the platform, what they own, when they were last seen.
  • AI spending — by app and by individual, which is how you answer “which of the 300 students used the budget” without building a special report.
  • Health & risk — apps that have crashed, certificates expiring in the next 90 days, security problems found in things apps depend on, and anything running on an old blueprint.
  • Activity log — who did what, when. An administrator acting on somebody else’s app has to give a reason, which is recorded and shown to the owner.

It is deliberately plain: tables, filters, and a queue. It is a tool for the handful of people running the platform, not something anyone else will ever see.

10 — On one laptop

All of it runs on a MacBook, with the internet switched off

Not a demonstration version. The actual platform — building, databases, sign-in, AI models, web addresses, certificates — on one machine, offline, from a fresh copy.

This is a working requirement rather than a nice touch, and it earns its cost three times over. A new developer gets to a working system on day one instead of week three. UBC has made no decision yet about where this will eventually run, so nothing can be allowed to depend on a particular answer. And anything demonstrable on a laptop can be demonstrated in a meeting room with unreliable wifi.

“Offline” means offline after a one-time setup that downloads what is needed. After that, everything runs with the network off. Adding a brand-new software dependency needs the internet — a stated limitation, not a surprise.

What a laptop cannot prove

  • Real CWL sign-in. Only UBC can register an app for that. A laptop proves the plumbing, never the registration.
  • Privacy approval. A human process at UBC. Locally it can be marked as overridden, which is recorded as an override rather than quietly passed.
  • Behaviour at scale. A laptop cannot tell you what happens with 5,000 people.
  • The quality of AI-written code. The small model that runs locally is there to prove the wiring works, not to write good software. Pointing at a proper model is a single configuration change — which is exactly what that arrangement was designed to buy.

11 — Glossary

Words that appear on this page

Blueprint
The prepared starting shape every new app is built from. One to begin with; more later, without redesigning anything.
Sandbox
A private workspace where the AI builds. Only its owner can see it, and it disappears when it is done.
Test copy
A real running version at a real address, reachable from inside UBC only, using invented test users rather than real people.
Live
The version students use. Real CWL sign-in, real data, and the only one requiring UBC registration and privacy approval.
CWL
Campus-Wide Login — the UBC username everyone already has.
Sign-in registration
The formal request that lets a specific live app use CWL. Manual, done by UBC, two to four weeks. One per app, every time.
Privacy assessment
UBC’s formal review of what personal information an app handles and why. Also one per live app. Manifest writes the first draft.
Manifest address
The permanent web address every app has from the day it is created. It never changes and never goes away.
Custom address
A departmental address such as labs.chem.ubc.ca, added alongside the permanent one once DNS proves it belongs to you.
DNS record
The entry that tells the internet where a web address points. Created by whoever runs the department’s domain, not by the faculty member.
Certificate
What makes an address show a padlock rather than a warning. Issued automatically, and watched so it never expires unnoticed.
Settings file
The short file travelling with each app that says what it needs — a database, sign-in, an AI model. Written by the AI, read by Manifest, reviewed before launch.
Token
What somebody’s own AI uses to work with Manifest from their own laptop. Covers the whole build loop and none of the four consequential actions.
Sleeping
An app nobody has used for a while stops running and starts again when somebody visits, so it costs nothing in between.
